# The quick win conversation

A 15 minute talk track for a first real conversation with a client or prospect. Eight beats. Each one has something to say and something to ask. The asking matters more than the saying.

The whole conversation is about their transformation outcomes. Tools appear once, and only as the foundation those outcomes get built on.

**This doc and the website are one story, but they are not the same length.** Beats 1 to 7 are published, in this order, at [mycio.co.nz/the-case.html](https://mycio.co.nz/the-case.html).

The page is deliberately sparse. It carries the headline of each beat, the visual, and the question, and almost nothing else. **The words in this doc are the ones you say. The words on the page are the ones they read while you say them.** If the page repeated this doc, it would compete with you in the room, and a prospect reading ahead has stopped listening.

So: talk the track cold, or scroll the page while you talk it, or send the link afterwards. The page will not say it as fully as you did, and that is the point. It says it in the same order, with the same questions, and leaves the room for you. If you change the argument in either place, change it in both.

**Name the two frameworks out loud.** The page now carries both as drawn diagrams, and they are the most repeatable thing we have. **The Climb** is how we get them from where they are to where they are going: Discover, Stabilise, Standardise, Transform, with security wrapped around all four rather than bolted on as a fifth. **The Flywheel** is what happens at the top, and it is the answer to "so what do we pay you for once it is fixed?": Strategy sets direction, Transformation delivers it, Operations keeps it turning, then it goes again. Use the names. A framework someone can draw on a napkin after the meeting is worth more than a page of explanation they cannot.

The page carries three things the talk track does not, because a cold reader needs them and a person sitting opposite you does not:

- **A provocative opening line.** The page leads on *"Is your IT managed? Quiet on results?"* Two questions, and the trap is in the order: almost everyone says yes to the first, which is exactly what leaves them exposed on the second. That is not how you open a meeting, because in the room you open on their business (beat 1). It is the right line for everything that has to earn attention in ten seconds: an email subject, a LinkedIn post, a first line at an event, the answer to "so what do you actually do?"
- **An opening recognition section** (`#recognise`) before beat 1, listing six things that have changed in their business, including the two people complain about most: proposals full of brand names, and an AI conversation that never ships anything. It qualifies without naming a size, a role or a sector, because our people arrive from all of those. Useful in the room too, when someone opens with "I'm not sure we're your kind of client".
- **Who turns up** (`#people`), near the close. Two names, one line each. In the room that is you, so it is redundant.
- **The form itself** (`#book`), so a convinced reader never has to go and find a contact page. Three lines beside it take the risk out: thirty minutes, no slide deck; an honest read on where you stand; if we are not the right fit, we say so. Those are worth saying out loud in the room too.
- **A standing "Book a call" button** in the sticky strip, and a prompt in the middle for anyone convinced before the end.

Between beats 3 and 4 the page pauses on a single line with nothing else on the screen: **"You bought a service. You needed a decision."** That is the hinge of the whole argument. In the room, it is the sentence to slow down on and then stop talking. Let them sit in it before you move to what you own.

## 1. Open on what drives their business, not on what breaks their IT

**Say:** Before we talk about anything technical, I want to understand what helps drive your business forward. Not the IT roadmap. The business.

**Ask:** If one thing worked properly in the next twelve months, what would make the biggest difference to you?

Write the answer down in front of them. Everything after this points back at it, and beat 8 hands it straight back to them.

---

## 2. Name the gap they already feel

**Say:** Three things have to happen in any business. Something breaks, and someone fixes it. Something needs building, and someone builds it to the brief. And something needs deciding: whether it is worth doing at all, and what comes next. Most businesses your size are well covered on the first two and have nobody at all on the third.

**Ask:** When something technical needs deciding, who decides it?

The answer is almost always "me, and I'm guessing". That is the gap. Let them say it rather than saying it for them.

---

## 3. Explain why what they are buying today cannot close that gap

This beat is new, and it is the one that does the most work. Do not skip it to get to the pitch. Until they see that the gap is structural rather than a matter of their provider trying harder, every alternative you offer sounds like a swap.

**Say:** The managed services model is not broken. It was just never built to do this part. Four reasons, and none of them are about your provider being bad at their job.

1. **The contract buys availability, not progress.** It commits to response times, uptime, and a monthly fee. There is not a line in it that says the business should be further ahead in twelve months.
2. **The margin sits inside the stack.** When the people advising on the tools are the people reselling the tools, the advice is never fully free. That is not dishonesty, it is just not independence, and independence is what you are short of.
3. **Volume is the business model.** Profitability needs hundreds of clients served from one pool of people. Attention goes to whoever escalates loudest. Nobody in that pool is sitting with your numbers thinking about your next three years.
4. **Nothing ever gets switched off.** A service that only adds is a service that only grows. Nobody in that arrangement is paid to tell you a project is not worth doing.

Then land it: you bought a service. What you needed was someone accountable for the direction. Those are different purchases.

**Ask:** In the last year, what has your provider talked you out of?

Usually nothing. That silence is the whole argument, and it is cleaner than anything you could say.

**Tone warning.** This beat only works if it stays fair. Attack the model, never the incumbent. If you sound like you are running down another provider, you become another provider running someone down. "They are probably good at what they sell" is a real sentence, say it and mean it.

---

## 4. Explain the ownership split, because it is the product

**Say:** You own everything. Your systems and your data, every subscription and licence in your name rather than resold through ours, every vendor contract signed by you, the documentation and the standards and the roadmap, and the capability, because we work beside your team rather than above them.

We own the method and the change. The direction and every decision that sets it. The change itself, discovery to handover, never delegated. The vendors, held to what they actually signed. The risk picture, live, in plain language for your board. And the outcome: if it does not land, that is ours to answer for.

Licensing and managed services go through at cost plus a transparent margin. If we ever part ways, you keep the lot and nothing gets held hostage.

**Ask:** How much of what you pay for today can you actually see inside?

This is where "not just another IT bill" earns its keep. Do not claim it, demonstrate it.

---

## 5. Walk them up the route so they see a path, not a project list

**Say:** Four stages and they run in order, each one resting on the last. Discover, so we map the business end to end before touching anything. Stabilise, so the noise stops. Standardise, so things stop being one-offs held together by one person who knows how. Then Transform, which is where the real gains live and why the first three matter.

Security is not one of the stages. It is the condition every stage has to meet, from day one. If someone sells you security as a phase you finish, they have told you something about how they work.

Map before we touch anything is the important bit. It is also how we price risk honestly rather than guessing, and it is why we will not quote a monthly number before we have done it.

**Ask:** Which of those four do you think you are sitting in right now?

Their answer tells you where the engagement starts and how long the runway is.

---

## 6. Settle the tools question so they can stop having it

**Say:** Microsoft, Fortinet, Veeam, Huntress and SentinelOne. Microsoft is the platform you already own and mostly underuse. Fortinet holds the network and the edge. Veeam means the business comes back when something goes wrong. Huntress and SentinelOne mean real people hunting threats on your machines, not another dashboard sending you alerts at 2am.

None of that is the point though. It is the floor. It exists so the ground stops moving under you, and so everything we build after it has something solid to sit on. Most providers sell you those five as the destination and bill you monthly for owning them. We treat them as the floor. Once the floor is solid and boring, you stop spending attention on tools and start compounding real gains on top.

**Ask:** How much of your technology conversation today is about tools rather than about where your business is heading?

---

## 7. Prove value, and be willing to kill work

**Say:** Getting things done is easy to buy. Getting the right things done, the ones that are right for your business, is the harder job and it is the one we take. So every piece of work carries a reason it was chosen and evidence of what it delivered. Some of the time that means we will tell you not to do something, or to wait. That is the clearest signal we are selecting work rather than just billing it.

One client saved over $500k in a single competitive vendor review. The first thing most owners notice when we come in is vendors that finally answer to someone.

**Ask:** Has anyone you have paid ever told you not to spend the money?

---

## 8. Close on one thing worth ninety days

**Say:** Let's not sign a transformation programme today. Pick the one thing you named at the start. Give us a short discovery, we will map it, and we will deliver one visible win inside the first thirty days. Visible to the business, not just to IT. If that lands, we keep climbing together.

**Ask:** What is the one thing?

## Notes for the room

**Lead with the question, not the credentials.** The opening ask does more work than any slide.

**Beat 3 earns beat 4.** If you are short on time, cut depth elsewhere and keep beat 3. Without it, the ownership split sounds like a feature list. With it, the split is the obvious answer to a problem they have just agreed they have.

**Do not quote a monthly number before the map.** Pricing without discovery is the thing every provider they have already tried did.

**Keep the five names short.** One line each, then move straight back to the foundation point. If they want depth on any of them, that is a good sign and a second conversation.

**Security is a condition, not a stage.** Four stages, wrapped in security. This matches the curve on How we work and the route on The case. Do not add a fifth "Secure" stage in conversation, because the moment it is a stage it is something that can be finished, deferred or descoped.

**Never out-jargon the people you are criticising.** The jargon line only works while we are cleaner than they are. The moment we answer "sick of buzzwords" with a buzzword of our own, we have joined them and the prospect can feel it even if they cannot name it. So: no invented categories, no platform names for things that are actually a way of working, and no leading with a product. If AI comes up, say what it did, not what it is called.

**Lead on the difference, not the service.** Every provider they have met says they are different. Almost none of them can name the thing. Ours is nameable in one sentence: everything stays in your name, and we own the decisions rather than the tickets. Say the sentence, then prove it with beat 4, rather than claiming "different" and leaving it there.

**Speak to the person carrying it, whoever they are.** Owner, CFO, board member, GM: the one thing they have in common is that the technology decision reached them and they are not the right person to make it. Aim at that, not at a job title. The page does the same, which is why it never names a role, a headcount or a sector.

**It is a stage, not a size.** They are not a prospect because they have 60 staff. They are a prospect because they have outgrown the arrangement that got them here. That is what qualifies, and it is the fastest way to open with someone who thinks they are too small or too big for us.

**Language to hold to:** what helps drive your business forward; the right work for your business; delivers value; moves your business forward. Not another IT company. Not another IT bill.

**Language to avoid:** anything that sounds like a managed services pitch, anything that leads with a product, any monthly-per-seat framing in the first conversation, and em-dashes in anything that ends up written down.

## Leaving it behind

Send the link to [mycio.co.nz/the-case.html](https://mycio.co.nz/the-case.html) rather than a deck. Same beats, same order, same questions, in about two minutes of reading.

It is short on purpose. Whoever was not in the room gets the shape of the argument and the questions it turns on, which is what travels to a board or a business partner.
